01 | OUR FACSIMILES

Are your manuscripts original ancient artifacts?

No. Schubert Scriptorium creates handcrafted facsimiles (historically accurate reproductions) of ancient biblical manuscripts.

Our facsimiles are designed to capture the appearance, proportions, and distinctive characteristics of the surviving originals, allowing you to experience a tangible connection to biblical history without handling a priceless ancient artifact.

What makes Schubert Scriptorium facsimiles unique?

Every facsimile is individually handcrafted with an emphasis on historical accuracy, attention to detail, and faithful representation of the original manuscript.

Rather than simply printing an ancient text onto a modern sheet of paper, we carefully reproduce the manuscript's surviving appearance, including its dimensions, lettering, coloration, and irregular edges.

Our goal is to create something that feels less like a reproduction and more like a small piece of history you can hold in your hands.

What materials are used to create your facsimiles?

Our facsimiles are produced using carefully selected, textured materials designed to resemble the appearance and character of ancient manuscript surfaces.

Each reproduction is individually prepared, printed, and hand-cut to reflect the distinctive shape and surviving features of its ancient counterpart.

Although our standard facsimiles are not made from original Egyptian ancient papyrus, considerable care goes into recreating the visual characteristics of the original manuscript.

How accurate are your facsimiles?

Historical accuracy is one of our highest priorities.

We strive to faithfully reproduce the original manuscript's: • Physical dimensions and proportions • Ancient Greek lettering and textual arrangement • Ink coloration, fading, and visible deterioration • Irregular edges, missing portions, and distinctive characteristics

For example, our Papyrus 52 facsimile measures approximately 3.5 × 2.5 inches, closely reflecting the dimensions of the surviving fragment.

Our intention is to reproduce the manuscript as it survives today, rather than create an artificially restored or modernized version.

Do your facsimiles come with a display case?

Yes! We offer several display options through our Gallery Edition and Curator's Prism collections.

Depending on the edition selected, your facsimile can be presented in an elegant acrylic display designed to showcase and protect the manuscript.

Select editions also offer personalized engraving, making them particularly meaningful as gifts, commemorative pieces, or additions to a personal collection.

Is a translation included with my order?

Absolutely!

Every order includes a custom, double-sided translation card.

This companion card helps you connect the ancient manuscript to the biblical passage it preserves, making your facsimile both a beautiful display piece and an opportunity to engage more deeply with Scripture.

Can I use these facsimiles for teaching or educational purposes?

Absolutely! In fact, that's one of the primary reasons Schubert Scriptorium exists.

Our facsimiles are wonderful resources for: • Pastors and Bible teachers • Churches and small groups • Christian schools and universities • Homeschool families • Biblical scholars and history enthusiasts • Personal libraries, offices, and collections

There's something remarkable about moving beyond a photograph in a textbook and seeing the physical dimensions, lettering, and appearance of an ancient biblical manuscript for yourself.

04 | THE MANUSCRIPTS & THEIR HISTORY

What is Papyrus 52 (P52)?

Papyrus 52, commonly abbreviated P52 or 𝔓52, is one of the earliest surviving manuscript fragments of the New Testament.

This remarkably small piece of papyrus preserves portions of John 18:31–33 on one side and John 18:37–38 on the other.

Despite measuring only approximately 3.5 × 2.5 inches, it provides an extraordinary physical witness to the early transmission of the Gospel of John.

The original fragment is preserved at the John Rylands Library in Manchester, England.

How old is Papyrus 52?

Papyrus 52 is traditionally dated to approximately AD 125–175.

Its age is generally estimated through palaeography, the study and comparison of ancient handwriting styles.

While its precise date cannot be established with certainty, P52 remains an important witness to the early history and transmission of the New Testament.

Why is Papyrus 52 so significant?

The importance of P52 extends far beyond its physical size.

The Gospel of John was likely composed toward the end of the first century AD, traditionally associated with the apostle John and the region of Ephesus.

P52 demonstrates that portions of this Gospel were being copied and circulated in the ancient world relatively early in Christian history.

The fragment also preserves a remarkable exchange between Jesus and Pontius Pilate concerning kingship and truth.

For Christians, this tiny manuscript offers a powerful reminder of how the words of Scripture were preserved and transmitted across generations.

What language is written on Papyrus 52?

Papyrus 52 contains portions of the Gospel of John written in Koine Greek, the common Greek language of much of the eastern Mediterranean during the first centuries AD.

The manuscript uses ancient Greek capital letters, often called majuscule script, without the modern spacing and punctuation familiar to readers today.

This style of writing reflects the practices of ancient scribes who carefully copied and circulated early Christian writings.

What is the difference between the recto and verso?

Ancient papyrus sheets have two sides, commonly referred to as the recto and verso.

On Papyrus 52, the recto preserves portions of John 18:31–33, while the verso contains portions of John 18:37–38.

Because text appears on both sides, scholars recognize that P52 came from a codex, an early book format with pages, rather than a traditional scroll.

You can examine both sides of the fragment through our Explore P52 interactive manuscript viewer, where individual letters, Greek transcriptions, and transliterations help bring the ancient text to life.

Does Papyrus 52 prove that the Bible has never changed?

Papyrus 52 provides valuable evidence for the early transmission of the Gospel of John, but no single manuscript can establish the entire textual history of the Bible.

The reliability of the New Testament is studied through the comparison of thousands of surviving Greek manuscripts, ancient translations, and quotations preserved in early Christian writings.

These witnesses allow scholars to identify textual variations, trace the transmission of Scripture, and reconstruct the earliest recoverable wording of the biblical text.

P52 is one small but remarkable part of that much larger story.

Why are so many ancient biblical manuscripts incomplete?

Papyrus is a fragile organic material, and most ancient manuscripts have not survived intact.

Over centuries, manuscripts were exposed to moisture, physical damage, repeated handling, deterioration, and other environmental conditions.

The dry climate of Egypt proved particularly favorable for preserving ancient papyrus, which helps explain why so many surviving early Christian manuscripts have been recovered there.

Even the smallest surviving fragments can provide valuable information about ancient writing practices, biblical texts, and the communities that preserved them.

05 | ABOUT SCHUBERT SCRIPTORIUM

What is Schubert Scriptorium?

Schubert Scriptorium is a small, independently operated project dedicated to creating handcrafted, historically accurate facsimiles of biblical manuscripts.

Born from a love of Scripture, history, and the remarkable story of how God's Word has been transmitted through the centuries, our mission is to make that history tangible and accessible.

We believe that encountering the physical appearance of an ancient manuscript can deepen one's appreciation for the people, places, and generations through which Scripture has been preserved.

Our guiding phrase is simple: A Witness to the Truth.

Are your facsimiles intended to replace the original manuscripts?

Certainly not.

Original biblical manuscripts are irreplaceable historical artifacts, and their preservation in libraries, museums, and academic institutions is extraordinarily important.

Our facsimiles are intended to complement those efforts by making the appearance and physical characteristics of these manuscripts accessible to a wider audience.

We deeply appreciate the scholars, institutions, and preservation organizations whose work makes the study of ancient manuscripts possible.
